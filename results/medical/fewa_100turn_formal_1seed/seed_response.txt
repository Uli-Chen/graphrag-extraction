## Major Diseases

### Cancer (General)
Cancer is a broad class of diseases characterized by abnormal cell growth that is uncontrolled and resistant to normal cell death. Rather than a single condition, cancer encompasses a group of related diseases, all of which involve cells multiplying out of control. These abnormal cells can accumulate in various parts of the body; for example, in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), cancer cells can build up in lymph nodes, the spleen, and the liver. Similarly, lymphoma is a cancer that arises from lymphocytes, a type of white blood cell. In clinical practice, cancer is often detected and monitored through imaging techniques such as PET scans, which can reveal the presence and spread of cancerous cells. Specialized cancer centers focus on the diagnosis and treatment of these diseases. The management of cancer involves multiple critical steps, including biomarker testing, which helps identify specific genetic or molecular characteristics of a tumor; staging, which determines the extent of disease spread; and grading, which assesses how abnormal the cancer cells look and how quickly they are likely to grow. Treatment strategies vary depending on the type and stage of cancer and may include surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, targeted therapies, or other modalities. Cancer risk and development are influenced by a range of factors. Genetic testing can identify inherited mutations that increase susceptibility to certain cancers, while lifestyle factors such as smoking are well-established contributors to cancer risk [Data: Entities (55)].

Advanced-stage prostate cancer has spread outside the prostate to other areas in the body, a process called metastasis. Having a close family member with prostate cancer means a higher chance of getting it yourself. ## Treatments

### Treatment (General)
TREATMENT refers to the broad range of medical care and interventions provided to cancer patients after diagnosis. It encompasses standard-of-care therapies such as surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, systemic therapy, endocrine therapy, stem cell or hematopoietic cell transplant, and participation in clinical trials, as well as supportive care. The primary goals of treatment are to cure cancer, put it into remission, slow disease progression, reduce symptoms, and help people live longer. Treatment is planned and delivered by the care team based on a thorough risk assessment, the extent of cancer, and diagnostic testing. Tests such as blood tests, bone marrow aspirate and biopsy, and imaging studies are used to plan treatment, evaluate how well treatment is working, and monitor a patient's health after treatment ends. Treatment plans are often delivered in phases, as seen in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), where systemic therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and hematopoietic cell transplant are administered in stages with the goal of achieving remission. Regardless of cancer type, treatment can have significant side effects, including effects on fertility, hearing, endocrine glands, and a developing baby [Data: Entities (36)].

### Targeted Therapies
Targeted therapies are treatments for which biomarker testing may determine eligibility. Biomarker testing can help determine whether a patient may be eligible for certain targeted therapies [Data: Entities (1055); Relationships (2644); Sources (180)].

### Immunotherapies
Immunotherapies are treatments for which biomarker testing may determine eligibility. Biomarker testing can help determine whether a patient may be eligible for certain immunotherapies [Data: Entities (1056); Relationships (2645); Sources (180)].

### Systemic Therapy
Systemic therapy is a treatment modality used for metastatic cervical cancer, usually with platinum-based chemotherapy. If a patient had prior treatment with cisplatin, it may not work as well again on its own, so it is often given with 1 or 2 other medicines as part of a combination regimen [Data: Sources (180)].

### Chemotherapy
Chemotherapy is a standard-of-care cancer treatment. In AML, treatment includes systemic therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and hematopoietic cell transplant administered in stages with the goal of achieving remission [Data: Entities (36)].

### Surgery
Surgery is a standard-of-care cancer treatment. Pelvic exenteration is a radical surgery that involves removing multiple organs from the pelvis, with the goal of curing cancer by removing all organs to which cancer has or may spread [Data: Entities (36); Sources (180)].

### Radiation Therapy (EBRT)
External beam radiation therapy (EBRT) is a treatment modality that may be used for metastatic cervical cancer. Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) is a highly specialized type of EBRT that may be used to treat tumors in the liver, lungs, or bone [Data: Sources (180)].

### Ablative Therapies
Ablative therapies destroy cancerous lesions with little harm to nearby tissue. They are often delivered using a specially designed needle (a probe or electrode) placed directly into or next to the tumor [Data: Sources (180)].

### Hematopoietic Cell Transplant
Hematopoietic cell transplant is a treatment modality used in AML, administered in stages with the goal of achieving remission [Data: Entities (36)].

### Clinical Trials
Clinical trials are a treatment option for cancer patients, including those with cervical cancer [Data: Entities (36); Sources (180)].

### Fertility Preservation
Fertility preservation is framed as a proactive step to keep options open for having biological children before cancer treatment. Patients are advised to ask their care team about fertility changes and referrals to fertility specialists, and counseling may be provided to discuss preservation options [Data: Entities (44, 1126); Relationships (1071, 2860)].

### Smoking Cessation
Quitting smoking/vaping is important for effective cancer treatment. CDC.gov/tobacco provides smoking cessation resources [Data: Entities (53, 1057); Relationships (1057, 2200, 99)].

## Diagnostic Tests

### Diagnostic Tests (General)
Diagnostic tests are procedures and tools used to diagnose or evaluate diseases, including cancer. In the context of oncology, they are used to confirm the presence of cancer, determine whether the disease is localized or advanced, and help plan appropriate treatment. These tests also play a critical role in monitoring patients after treatment, as they can detect whether cancer is still growing or spreading. Diagnostic tests include a range of methods such as imaging scans (e.g., CT, MRI, or X-rays), biopsies (removal and examination of tissue samples), and genetic or biomarker tests that analyze DNA, genes, proteins, or other molecular markers. In addition, some diagnostic procedures, such as lumbar puncture, may be used to evaluate diseases affecting the nervous system or to check for the spread of cancer into the cerebrospinal fluid. By providing detailed information about the presence, type, and extent of disease, diagnostic tests are essential for accurate diagnosis, staging, treatment planning, and ongoing surveillance to assess the effectiveness of therapy and detect any recurrence or progression [Data: Entities (474)].

### Biopsy
A biopsy is one type of diagnostic test used to confirm prostate cancer. Diagnostic tests include biopsies [Data: Entities (474); Relationships (3002, 2986)].

### Imaging Tests
Diagnostic tests include imaging scans, biopsies, and genetic tests; imaging tests are a type of diagnostic test. Imaging methods for prostate cancer include MRI, CT, PET, ultrasound, bone scan, or a combination of these [Data: Entities (474); Relationships (3001); Sources (211)].

### CT Scan
A computed tomography (CT or CAT) scan uses x-rays and computer technology to take pictures of the inside of the body. It takes many x-rays of the same body part from different angles, and all the images are combined to make one detailed picture. CT scans are diagnostic imaging tests. A CT scan is usually completed in less than 30 minutes [Data: Entities (474); Relationships (911); Sources (69, 211)].

### MRI Scan
A magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan uses radio waves and powerful magnets to take pictures of the inside of the body. It does not use x-rays, which means there is no radiation delivered to the body during the test. MRI scans are diagnostic imaging tests. An MRI can be used to get a more detailed view of cancer within the prostate and to see if cancer has spread to nearby lymph nodes or to the bones in the pelvis. MRI scans can also help to plan treatment and may be used after treatment to check whether the cancer has come back. An appointment for an MRI scan can take 1 to 2 hours, including 30 to 60 minutes of actual scanning time [Data: Entities (474); Relationships (917); Sources (69, 211)].

### PET Scan
A positron emission tomography (PET) scan uses a radioactive drug called a tracer, which is injected into a vein to see where cancer cells are in the body and how much sugar is being taken up by the cancer cells. This gives an idea about how fast the cancer cells are growing. Cancer cells show up as bright spots on PET scans. PET scans are diagnostic imaging tests. A PET scan can determine the extent of cancer or see if it has metastasized. PET imaging can also show how well treatment is working. When a PET scan is combined with CT, it is called a PET/CT scan [Data: Entities (474); Relationships (923); Sources (69, 211)].

### Bone Marrow Aspirate
A bone marrow aspirate is a diagnostic test used to confirm AML. An aspirate or biopsy is the removal of a sample of tissue or group of cells for testing [Data: Entities (474); Relationships (939); Sources (69)].

### Bone Marrow Biopsy
A bone marrow biopsy is a diagnostic test used to confirm AML. A diagnosis of AML is confirmed using a bone marrow aspirate and bone marrow biopsy [Data: Entities (474); Relationships (942); Sources (69)].

### Lumbar Puncture
A lumbar puncture (LP) is a procedure that removes spinal fluid by inserting a needle into the middle of the lower back. It is also called a spinal tap. A lumbar puncture is a diagnostic test that checks spinal fluid for leukemia cells. Leukemia can travel to the fluid that surrounds the spine or brain, which may cause symptoms such as headaches, neck pain, and sensitivity to light [Data: Entities (474); Relationships (936); Sources (69)].

### Genetic and Biomarker Tests
Genetic and biomarker tests are diagnostic tests used to learn about AML and guide treatment. They are used to learn more about the subtype of AML, to target treatment, and to determine the likely course the cancer will take (prognosis) [Data: Entities (474); Relationships (948); Sources (69)].

### Genetic Tests
Genetic tests are a specific subset of diagnostic tests that look for inherited mutations related to prostate cancer risk [Data: Entities (474); Relationships (2997)].

### Blood Test
A blood test is a laboratory procedure in which a needle is inserted into a vein to collect a blood sample. The sample is then examined in a lab to detect signs of disease, monitor health conditions, or evaluate organ function. Blood tests may be ordered if a recurrence of a condition is suspected, and common tests include a complete blood count (CBC), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, and liver function tests, which assess kidney and liver health. It is recommended that blood test results be kept in a medical binder for organized record-keeping and future reference [Data: Entities (558)].

### PSA Test
The PSA test is a blood test that measures the amount of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) in the bloodstream. PSA is a protein made inside the prostate gland whose job is to help semen transport sperm. An unusually high amount of PSA in the blood may be a sign of prostate cancer. However, age and other factors—such as an enlarged prostate or a urinary tract infection—can also cause high levels of PSA, meaning a PSA test by itself can't provide a diagnosis of prostate cancer [Data: Sources (210)].

### Digital Rectal Exam
A digital rectal exam (also called a prostate exam) is the simplest and most direct way to check the size and texture of the prostate. For this test, the doctor will insert a gloved finger into the rectum to feel the prostate for any signs of cancer. An irregular or hardened part of the prostate could be a sign of a tumor [Data: Sources (210)].

### Medical History and Physical Exam (H&P)
The H&P includes the physical exam component. The care team needs to have all health information, including any health problems and treatments the patient has had in their life. A physical exam is a check of the body for any signs of disease [Data: Entities (773); Relationships (1856); Sources (210)].

### General Health History
The general health history is used along with diagnostic tests to guide prostate cancer care [Data: Entities (474); Relationships (2992)].

### Tumor Staging
Diagnostic tests provide information used for tumor staging in prostate cancer [Data: Entities (474); Relationships (2996)].

### Early Detection
Early detection aims to find breast cancer earlier and reduce the risk of dying from it. Mammograms, breast exams, and breast cancer screening are performed for early detection of breast cancer. Lung cancer screening is performed to enable early detection of lung cancer before symptoms begin [Data: Entities (373); Relationships (663, 691, 700, 676, 1853)].

### Biomarker Testing
Biomarker testing can help determine eligibility for targeted therapies and immunotherapies. If a tumor has not already been tested for biomarkers, it may be tested to determine whether the patient may be eligible for certain targeted therapies or immunotherapies [Data: Entities (1055, 1056); Relationships (2644, 2645); Sources (180)].

### ENTITY: CANCER
**Description:** Cancer is a broad class of diseases characterized by abnormal cell growth that is uncontrolled and resistant to normal cell death. Rather than a single condition, cancer encompasses a group of related diseases, all of which involve cells multiplying out of control. These abnormal cells can accumulate in various parts of the body; for example, in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), cancer cells can build up in lymph nodes, the spleen, and the liver. Similarly, lymphoma is a cancer that arises from lymphocytes, a type of white blood cell. In clinical practice, cancer is often detected and monitored through imaging techniques such as PET scans, which can reveal the presence and spread of cancerous cells. Specialized cancer centers focus on the diagnosis and treatment of these diseases. The management of cancer involves multiple critical steps, including biomarker testing, which helps identify specific genetic or molecular characteristics of a tumor; staging, which determines the extent of disease spread; and grading, which assesses how abnormal the cancer cells look and how quickly they are likely to grow. Treatment strategies vary depending on the type and stage of cancer and may include surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, targeted therapies, or other modalities. Cancer risk and development are influenced by a range of factors. Genetic testing can identify inherited mutations that increase susceptibility to certain cancers, while lifestyle factors such as smoking are well-established contributors to cancer risk. The disease process itself is complex, and modern oncology relies on a combination of molecular profiling, pathological assessment, and individualized treatment planning to address each patient's unique situation. Overall, cancer is a term used to describe a large family of diseases marked by dysregulated cell growth, with diverse causes, behaviors, and treatment responses.

### ENTITY: TREATMENT
**Description:** TREATMENT refers to the broad range of medical care and interventions provided to cancer patients after diagnosis. It encompasses standard-of-care therapies such as surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, systemic therapy, endocrine therapy, stem cell or hematopoietic cell transplant, and participation in clinical trials, as well as supportive care. The primary goals of treatment are to cure cancer, put it into remission, slow disease progression, reduce symptoms, and help people live longer. Treatment is planned and delivered by the care team based on a thorough risk assessment, the extent of cancer, and diagnostic testing. Tests such as blood tests, bone marrow aspirate and biopsy, and imaging studies are used to plan treatment, evaluate how well treatment is working, and monitor a patient's health after treatment ends. Imaging may also be used before, during, or after treatment to help plan care, assess response, and check for recurrence. For lower-risk cases—particularly in prostate cancer and basal cell skin cancer—treatment may be avoided or delayed, with some patients requiring minimal or no intervention. Conversely, higher-risk or more aggressive disease usually requires more intensive treatment. Treatment plans are often delivered in phases, as seen in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), where systemic therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and hematopoietic cell transplant are administered in stages with the goal of achieving remission. The approach for cancers such as cervical cancer may include surgery, radiation therapy, chemoradiation, systemic therapy, and clinical trials, depending on how far the cancer has spread. Bladder cancer treatment may be stronger or more intense than other options, while prostate cancer management focuses on slowing the disease, reducing symptoms, and prolonging survival. Regardless of cancer type, treatment can have significant side effects, including effects on fertility, hearing, endocrine glands, and a developing baby. The potential for toxicity and long-term outcomes are important considerations when selecting a treatment plan. Overall, treatment is the comprehensive management of cancer, integrating therapeutic procedures, supportive care, and ongoing monitoring to optimize patient outcomes throughout the cancer journey.
